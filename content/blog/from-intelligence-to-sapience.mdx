<p className="lead">We have become so obsessed with intelligence. Seriously!</p>

Lately, it seems every new frontier model comes with increased reasoning, faster computation, larger
context windows, and impressive capabilities. Surprisingly, these machines can know a vast amount of
information and outperform humans at particular tasks but still fail to understand whether what they
are doing is worthwhile or explain the reasoning behind every decision they make. But perhaps what
we need isn't machines that are more intelligent, but ones that can exercise **sapience**. The
distinction between intelligence and sapience isn't universally agreed upon, and the terms are
sometimes used interchangeably. Sapience, as opposed to intelligence, in this essay, is used to
refer to the ability to create, understand, reason, exercise judgment, and deliberate about what is
good in addition to acquiring and processing information.

> **Being capable of doing something does not mean knowing whether it is worth doing.**

A machine could produce an impressive mathematical solution and still be pursuing an entirely wrong
objective. If we want to build machines that can do more than simply achieve the goals we give them,
we first need to understand what makes an action, or perhaps even a thing, good.

## What does it mean for something to be good?

In Aristotle's pursuit of a good life, he provided an operating manual that could attempt to answer
this question: the *Nicomachean Ethics*. Around 2,400 years ago, Aristotle argued that a good thing
is one that fulfils its unique function. A good watch is one that informs its owner of the correct
time when asked. A good carrier bag is one that enables someone to carry their luggage. A good knife
is one that enables someone to cut through material. But what does it mean for a human to be good?
And how could we apply the same concept to make a machine good? To understand what makes a human
good, we need to look at what is unique about humans: we have a soul whose function is to think and
feel. According to Aristotle, the ultimate form of an excellent soul reveals itself in a clear
intellect and a noble character. Although Aristotle was concerned with human knowledge and
reasoning, I think his distinctions offer an interesting starting point for considering what we might
want from a sapient machine. He identifies five intellectual virtues: **techne, episteme, nous,
sophia, and phronesis.**

---

## 1. Techne

*Knowledge and skill involved in producing something well.*

I have personally already seen impressive demonstrations of generative AI doing things. I have
experimented with Gemini CLI and Claude Code to write and debug code, and generative models can
already compose music and produce illustrations. Traditionally, getting this done would require
considerable human skill, effort and time. But I think there is a distinction between **generating
something and understanding the craft involved in producing it well.** (One might argue whether it's
really important to understand the craft of producing something well if you can produce it.) A
machine might generate a song that sounds pretty cool, but does it understand musical composition?
Does it understand how harmony, rhythm, melody and structure interact to create a particular effect?
Can it recognise why a composition works, identify its weaknesses and make deliberate improvements?
I do not know. What I think, though, is that a sapient machine should not only be capable of
producing things. It should develop a deeper understanding of the crafts behind them: why certain
methods work, when they are appropriate and what makes the resulting work good.

## 2. Episteme

*Systematic knowledge of things that can be demonstrated or understood through explanation.*

For machines, this would mean going beyond recognising patterns in data. Consider a machine
designing a bridge. It might produce a structurally sound design by identifying patterns in
thousands of existing bridges. However, a deeper form of knowledge (in my opinion) would involve
understanding the engineering principles governing structural integrity, material stress and load
distribution. It would need to explain why its design works, identify the assumptions behind its
calculations and recognise where its knowledge is insufficient.

## 3. Nous

*The intellect's grasp of fundamental principles that cannot simply be derived from other principles.*

For a machine to develop something resembling *nous*, it would need to do more than accumulate
observations. It would need to recognise the fundamental principles that underpin its knowledge and
use them as a foundation for further reasoning.

## 4. Sophia

*Theoretical wisdom, combining nous and episteme to understand fundamental truths.*

This is my favourite and the most interesting of these virtues. Aristotle describes it as
theoretical wisdom, combining *nous* and *episteme* to understand fundamental truths. If *episteme*
concerns systematic knowledge and *nous* concerns grasping fundamental principles, *sophia* brings
these together. It is not simply knowing more things, but understanding how knowledge and
fundamental principles relate to one another.

## 5. Phronesis

*Deliberating well about what is good and appropriate in particular circumstances.*

**Should a sapient machine be able to disagree with its creators when it reasons that their
objectives are wrong?** It might have the technical ability to design a system, the scientific
knowledge to understand how it works, and the theoretical understanding to recognise its broader
implications. But would it have the practical wisdom to question the objective itself?

---

The ability to create should be guided by knowledge. Knowledge should be examined through an
understanding of fundamental principles. Those principles should contribute to a broader
understanding of the world, and that understanding should inform practical judgment. Some current
machines possess some of these capabilities individually. They could build something without
understanding why it should be built, or understand the consequences of an action without knowing
how to deliberate about them. Perhaps sapience is not about possessing these virtues separately, but
combining them: **a system in which the ability to make, know, understand and judge works together
in pursuit of what is good.** I don't want to claim that Aristotle's framework provides a blueprint
for building such machines. Rather, I think it offers a useful starting point for asking a question
that seems increasingly important as AI develops:

<p className="closing-question">
  <strong>Should we be concerned only with how intelligent our machines become, or also with whether they can learn to exercise wisdom?</strong>
</p>

Let me know what you think!
